# POS Reporting and Financial Controls Readiness

Branch: `feature/pos-operations-v1`

This document defines what the current POS reports measure and what they do not establish. These are operational reports, not audited accounts, tax reports, bank reconciliation, inventory valuation, or profitability statements.

## Sales report

The sales report is scoped to one inventory location and an inclusive `from` / `to` date range. The query uses `LocalDateTime` boundaries: start date at 00:00 inclusive and the day after the end date at 00:00 exclusive. There is no timezone stored in these timestamps; confirm the deployed server timezone before relying on date cutoffs.

- **Transactions / subtotal / discounts / sales after discount:** completed POS sales whose sale creation timestamp falls inside the selected period. Sales after discount is the sum of sale grand totals before any later returns or voids.
- **Refunds:** completed customer returns plus completed full-sale voids processed during the selected period. They are grouped by the return/void processing date, not the original sale date.
- **Full void reversals:** completed records whose return type is `VOID` only. Ordinary customer returns are not full-sale reversals.
- **Recorded refund allocations:** refund tender allocations recorded in POS, grouped by method. This is not evidence that a noncash refund reached the customer's account.
- **Net after returns and voids:** sales after discount for sales created in the period minus all completed return/void refund totals processed in the period. Because sales and refunds are grouped by their own event dates, this is a period activity measure, not a cohort-based lifetime net value of the sales created in the same period. It can be negative in a period with refunds for older sales.
- **Exchange credit applied:** non-cash credit applied to replacement sales for exchanges created during the period. It is shown separately and is not included in refund tender totals. Do not add it to sales or subtract it again from net sales without a separate accounting policy.
- **Sales by payment method:** tender allocations recorded against completed sales in the period. UPI/card references are staff-entered operational references; they are not provider-confirmed transactions or bank-cleared funds.
- **Refunds by payment method:** tender allocations recorded on completed returns/voids processed during the period; provider settlement is not confirmed by this report.
- **Cashier totals:** completed sales attributed to the cashier on the sale record; later returns/voids are not allocated back to cashier totals.
- **Top products:** gross quantity and line total from sale items in the period. These figures do not allocate sale-level discounts, returns, voids, or exchange dispositions back to individual products.

The report currently does not calculate tax breakdowns, COGS, gross margin, operating expenses, net profit, receivables, payables, payment-provider fees, chargebacks, or bank settlement.

## Inventory report

The inventory report shows recorded on-hand, reserved, available, and low-stock quantities by variant at a location. It does not assign a monetary value to stock. Do not describe quantity multiplied by retail price as inventory valuation.

## Procurement cost report

The procurement cost report sums the unit costs stored on purchase-order lines for goods receipt quantities, then subtracts the same recorded unit costs for supplier-return quantities, based on receipt and supplier-return processing timestamps.

It is a **net recorded procurement-cost activity measure** only. It is not proof of supplier invoice amounts, amounts paid, supplier credit notes, accounts payable, landed cost, freight, tax, purchase discounts, inventory valuation, or COGS. Purchase order unit cost is entered operationally and is copied to receipt/return records; it is not independently matched to supplier invoices or payment records.

## Cash and payment controls

- Cash drawer expected balance is based on recorded cash movements only. UPI/card amounts do not belong in the cash drawer.
- Shift close and cash-refund operations both lock the shift row before changing drawer state. A MySQL concurrency regression test now races a cash refund against shift close and verifies that either the refund is included before close or is rejected after close, with no post-close drawer mutation.

- Cash-in/cash-out entries require a reason and are recorded against the shift with the acting username. A cashier may record movements on their own shift; a manager/admin may manage another staff member's shift. Cash-out is capped at the current expected drawer balance, but cash-in has no configured ceiling.
- There is no separate fresh-password approval, supervisor threshold, attachment/evidence field, or second-person confirmation for cash-in/cash-out. A large or unsupported cash-in can inflate expected drawer cash, while an otherwise permitted cash-out can remove cash without independent approval. The actor/reason audit trail is useful but does not prove the physical movement occurred.
- Before production, the owner must define cash-in/out limits, permitted reasons, approval requirements, evidence retention, and who may authorize movements on another cashier's shift. Do not invent monetary thresholds without that policy.
- UPI/card sale and refund references are operator-entered. Verify transactions in the merchant/provider portal independently.
- POS does not initiate or verify provider refunds. Complete and verify noncash refunds externally before recording them.
- Reconcile daily sales tender totals against provider transaction exports and provider net settlements against bank credits. Investigate fees, timing differences, reversals, chargebacks, and unmatched references.
- Exchange credit is a separate settlement component and must not be mistaken for a cash refund or provider refund.

## Required before financial/accounting use

1. Accountant-approved tax configuration and statutory invoice requirements.
2. Supplier invoice matching, credit-note handling, freight/tax/discount allocation, and landed-cost policy.
3. Provider payment/refund integration or documented manual reconciliation with evidence and exception ownership.
4. Bank settlement reconciliation, fees, chargebacks, and payment timing rules.
5. Inventory costing method and controlled opening-stock valuation.
6. COGS and returns accounting policy, including damaged/discarded stock and exchange treatment.
7. Explicit business timezone and financial-period boundaries.
8. Accounting review and test cases against representative real-world transactions.

Until those controls exist, label these screens as operational POS reports. Do not use them as audited financial statements or claim profit from them.


## POS idempotency-key scope and retention

- The database enforces uniqueness within each owning table, not across the entire POS domain: sales, returns, exchanges, shifts and cash movements each have their own unique idempotency-key constraint. This is appropriate for replay within each operation type, but it is not one global key registry.
- Checkout, return, exchange and shift keys are trimmed and limited to 100 characters by service validation, while the corresponding SQL columns allow 150 characters. Cash movement keys are also limited to 100 characters; internally generated movement keys add operation prefixes and suffixes but remain within the 150-character column limit.
- Exchange creates linked return and replacement-sale keys by prefixing the supplied exchange key (POS-EXCHANGE-RETURN: and POS-EXCHANGE-SALE:). Checkout accepts arbitrary caller-provided keys in the same pos_sales namespace. Therefore, a checkout key deliberately or accidentally matching a reserved exchange-generated key can block that exchange through the unique constraint. The current design relies on prefixes rather than a typed/global key namespace.
- Cash movement records also share one key namespace across manual cash-in/out and internally generated opening-float, cash-sale and refund movements. Replay validation checks shift, actor, movement type, amount and reason, which prevents a mismatched request from silently replaying, but a conflicting key still causes an operation to fail.
- There is no automatic TTL or cleanup job for POS idempotency records. These keys are stored on durable financial/operational records and should not be deleted independently: doing so can remove the database's replay guard and allow an old request to be processed again. Retention/deletion must be governed by an approved financial-record retention policy, with dependent records and backups considered together.
- Before production, reserve and validate internal prefixes for client-supplied keys (or introduce operation-specific key namespaces), test conflicting-key behavior, and document retention. Do not add automatic deletion of sales/returns/exchanges/cash movements merely to reduce table size.


## POS location isolation and multi-location authorization

- POS checkout is location-consistent at the transaction level: it locks the requested active inventory location, requires the selected open shift to belong to that location and the authenticated cashier, then sells stock against that location. Return/void and exchange similarly derive the source sale location and require the selected shift to belong to it. These checks prevent using a shift from another location for those transactions.
- The staff account model currently contains username, password, global role and enabled status only. It has no assigned-location relationship or permitted-location list. Consequently, the application does not enforce location-scoped staff access.
- Cashiers, managers and admins can list all active locations and request the catalog for any active location. The catalog intentionally includes the global active product/variant catalogue while showing stock quantities for the requested location.
- Managers/admins can query sales without a location filter (all locations), fetch a sale by ID, view its returns/exchanges, query shift history for any location, and request reports for any location. Manager/admin role is global rather than location-scoped. This is an authorization boundary gap if the business later operates multiple branches or grants managers access to only one branch.
- Register and shift operations validate register/location consistency for checkout, return and exchange, but opening a shift accepts any active register ID and there is no staff-to-register/location assignment. A cashier can therefore open a shift at any active location's register if they know its ID.
- No evidence of a current exploit against the single-shop deployment is established by this review. For the planned one-shop operation, this is a future multi-location readiness gap rather than a reason to invent branch restrictions. Before adding branches, add explicit staff-location assignments, enforce them in every location/sale/register/shift/report endpoint and service, scope queries by authorized location, and test cross-location ID substitution. Until then, do not treat this POS as securely partitioned for multiple branches.

## Online checkout, prescription data and tracking privacy review

The public online checkout and order-tracking paths were reviewed alongside the POS controls. This section records the current implementation limits; it does not claim these are all exploitable vulnerabilities.

- The checkout request DTO has no Bean Validation constraints and the controller does not use `@Valid`. The service validates that the cart is non-empty and that each item has positive product/variant IDs and quantity, and it verifies product/variant ownership and active status. It does not currently impose explicit maximum cart-line count, per-line quantity, aggregate quantity, or customer-field length limits at the request boundary. The database schema may reject some oversized values, but database errors are not a substitute for predictable API validation.
- Prescription input is accepted as a generic `Map<String,Object>`. The service allowlists the output eye fields and truncates individual values to 40 characters, which limits stored manual prescription values. However, there is no explicit request-level bound on the incoming JSON object size, nesting/field count, or serialized payload size before parsing and processing. Add limits at both HTTP/request and domain levels before exposing checkout to untrusted high-volume traffic.
- Image-mode prescription data stores a caller-supplied HTTPS URL and a caller-supplied filename string. The service checks only that the URL begins with `https://` and caps its length; it does not enforce an approved storage-host allowlist or verify that the object belongs to this shop's upload workflow. A user can therefore submit a URL on an arbitrary HTTPS host. This can cause privacy leakage to third-party hosts when staff/admin browsers load the image, and the filename must be treated as untrusted display text. Do not fetch these URLs from the backend; before production, use controlled private object storage, signed/authorized access, strict host and object ownership validation, safe content-type/size checks at upload, and a retention/deletion policy for prescription records.
- Public order tracking uses a high-entropy random token, which is materially stronger than sequential order IDs. Nevertheless, the tracking token is a bearer credential: anyone who obtains it can view the tracking response. Keep it out of analytics, referrers, public logs and support screenshots; avoid exposing more customer/order detail than necessary and provide a token rotation/revocation process if it is disclosed.
- The current order entity stores customer name, email, phone, address, city, state and pincode, plus prescription details on order items. These are personal and health-adjacent records. Access to admin order details must remain role-protected, and production needs defined retention, staff access, backup encryption, incident handling and deletion/legal-retention rules. Do not treat a public image URL as private merely because it is difficult to guess.
- The online checkout controller currently catches broad exceptions and writes their messages to standard error. Replace ad-hoc stderr logging with structured logging that excludes prescription contents, customer contact/address data, tokens and payment secrets; include a correlation/order identifier and sanitized exception class instead. Review reverse-proxy/application access logs for query strings and tracking-token leakage.
- No explicit application-level rate limit was found on the reviewed public checkout or tracking endpoints. Enforce edge/application throttling and request-body limits before production to reduce automated checkout spam, token probing and resource exhaustion. A rate limit must account for legitimate shared networks and should not be used as a substitute for high-entropy tracking tokens.

Technical work that can be implemented without inventing shop policy: request DTO validation, bounded request bodies/cart sizes, safe structured logging, and tests for malformed/oversized input. Decisions still required from the owner/client: approved private image-storage provider, prescription retention period, who may access prescription images, and the minimum customer fields exposed on public tracking.

## Eye-checkup appointment API review

- Public booking accepts the JPA `Appointment` entity directly rather than a dedicated request DTO. The controller checks that name, phone, date and time are present and that the date is not before the server's `LocalDate.now()`; it restricts booking to 09:00, 12:00 and 15:00. It does not explicitly validate name/phone/email lengths or formats, and there is no `@Valid` request-boundary validation. Oversized or malformed values may therefore fail later at persistence rather than return a consistent client error.
- The repository exposes `existsByAppointmentDateAndAppointmentTimeAndStatusNot(...)`, but the public booking method does not call it. Multiple customers can therefore book the same date/time slot. Whether that is a defect depends on the shop's real appointment capacity (one customer per slot vs multiple simultaneous checkups); confirm that rule before enforcing uniqueness. If capacity is one, enforce it transactionally with a database uniqueness strategy or a locked slot/capacity record, not only a pre-insert existence check that races under concurrent requests.
- Booking logs print the customer name and appointment details to standard output. Remove customer-identifying log lines and use structured, minimized operational logs.
- Admin status updates accept only the four expected values and the endpoint is protected by the admin route policy. However, status changes have no dedicated actor/time/reason audit record. Clearing all appointments requires the fresh-password `CLEAR_APPOINTMENTS` authorization token and is admin-only, which is a useful safeguard; the clear operation still has no durable deletion audit/snapshot of what was removed.
- The endpoint checks past dates against the server's local date. Production must set and consistently use the shop's explicit timezone so date validation and appointment scheduling do not shift around deployment/server timezone changes.
- No appointment-specific automated controller/service tests were found in the inspected test tree. Add tests for required fields, invalid/past dates, allowed slots, overlong values, duplicate/capacity behavior once the shop's slot-capacity rule is confirmed, and authorization of list/update/clear operations.

Implementation that does not require business policy: replace entity binding with a validated DTO, add explicit field bounds and normalized input handling, remove PII console logging, and add tests. Business decision required: maximum bookings per slot and the timezone governing appointment dates/times.

## Public catalogue and website-content API review

- The reviewed public GET routes are explicitly permitted in Spring Security: active product catalogue, product-by-ID, active marketing banners, current offer bar, website images, and order tracking. Admin mutation routes under `/api/admin/**` fall through to the admin-role rule; the admin content mutations are not anonymously exposed by the inspected security matcher configuration.
- Public product catalogue uses a dedicated `CustomerProductCatalogResponse.ProductItem` projection, which is preferable to exposing the product entity for the collection endpoint. However, public `GET /api/products/{id}` returns the JPA `Product` entity directly. That response includes internal/legacy fields such as `version`, `stockQuantity`, `createdAt`, and raw `specifications`; use a customer-safe DTO consistently so the public API contract does not accidentally expand when the entity changes. Verify that product-by-ID is actually needed by the frontend before retaining it.
- Public website content endpoints return entity objects directly. The inspected entities currently contain only public display fields and timestamps, so no direct credential/customer-data exposure was identified. Still, dedicated response DTOs would prevent future admin-only fields from leaking through public endpoints.
- Four reviewed controllers (products, marketing banners, offer bar and website images) declare controller-level `@CrossOrigin(origins = "http://localhost:5173")`, while `SecurityConfig` separately defines environment-driven global CORS origins through `app.cors.allowed-origins`. This duplication can make production CORS behavior inconsistent or unexpectedly restrictive. Remove the hardcoded controller annotations and keep one centrally managed CORS policy; verify actual browser preflight and GET/POST behavior using the production origin before release.
- Admin banner, offer-bar and website-image mutation endpoints bind JPA entities directly from request bodies and have no dedicated DTO validation. Database column limits provide only partial protection; add explicit validation for title/message lengths, URLs, placement values, display order, colors and image keys. Validate target URLs to prevent unsafe schemes such as `javascript:` and define whether only relative paths or approved HTTPS destinations are allowed. For banner placement, validate against the exact supported placement vocabulary used by the frontend.
- Offer-bar public selection filters active records and date windows in application memory after loading all active offers. If the table remains small this is not a current performance issue, but it should become a repository query with explicit ordering and a defined policy for overlapping active offers before content volume grows. The current `findByActiveTrue()` selection uses the first matching result without an explicit order.
- No direct public mutation exposure was found in the inspected Spring Security rules. Do not infer that from controller comments alone; retain automated anonymous/role-based API tests whenever these routes are changed.

Recommended implementation: remove duplicated hardcoded CORS annotations, add public-safe product/content response DTOs where the frontend contract permits, and validate admin content input with DTOs. No shop-specific financial policy is needed for these changes.

## Razorpay payment verification and webhook ingress review

- Checkout verification does not trust the browser's success flag: it verifies the Razorpay signature, locates the order by provider order ID, derives expected paise from the stored total, asks Razorpay to confirm capture/order/amount/currency, and delegates final confirmation to a transactional service. This is a sound layered design.
- `VerifyPaymentRequest` has no Bean Validation annotations and `/api/payment/verify` does not use `@Valid`. It checks only nonblank values before passing provider IDs/signature to verification. Add bounded lengths and format checks for provider order/payment IDs and the hex signature, plus a request-body size limit. Keep provider-specific formats aligned with Razorpay's documented contract rather than inventing overly strict patterns.
- The webhook controller requires signature, event ID and non-empty raw body, caps the body at 1 MiB, verifies the signature against raw bytes, and relies on a unique event ID for concurrency-safe duplicate delivery. The service validates event ID length (150), parses JSON and bounds event type (100), then stores the raw payload for asynchronous processing. These are good controls.
- The controller passes a trimmed event ID into the service, and the service rejects IDs over 150 characters. However, the request header itself has no explicit ingress/proxy bound, and signature length/format is not bounded before verification. Enforce reasonable header limits at the reverse proxy and application boundary while preserving the provider's actual signature format.
- The webhook controller returns a validation exception's message directly to the provider. Current messages are generic, but keep the response contract deliberately generic so future parser/provider details cannot leak. Unexpected webhook storage failures return 500, correctly allowing provider retry; add a sanitized structured error log with an internal correlation/event-record identifier without logging signature, raw payload or payment/customer data.
- Webhook processing logs only the internal event-record ID and exception class, not payload or payment identifiers. The event is durably stored before asynchronous processing, and the worker has lease/retry mechanics. The manual-review queue validates page size (1-100) and returns a DTO rather than raw payload JSON.
- Raw webhook payloads are retained as LONGTEXT with no documented retention/purge policy. They can contain customer/payment data. Define retention, encryption-at-rest/backup handling, restricted database access and safe deletion after the required reconciliation/audit period. Do not delete pending or manual-review events as routine cleanup.
- Payment verification currently returns the tracking token and tracking URL to the browser after successful payment. This is required by the customer flow but means browser storage, analytics and support tooling must treat the token as a secret. Do not log the full response or token.
- No concrete flaw was established in the core signature/capture confirmation sequence during this review. Remaining implementation tasks are bounded DTO/header validation, request-size enforcement for verification, generic webhook error responses, and safe failure logging. Retention and reverse-proxy limits require production configuration/policy decisions.

## POS reporting and shift-history API resource review

- POS sales history is paginated (page >= 0, size 1-100) and deterministically sorted by created time and ID. Webhook review is also paginated and caps page size. These are positive controls.
- POS shift history returns at most 100 rows, so it is bounded, but the API has no pagination/cursor and silently stops at the most recent 100. This is an operational limitation for older audit investigations; add pagination rather than raising the fixed cap.
- Sales reporting accepts a date range up to 367 calendar days, but the service loads all matching sales and returns into memory and then aggregates them in Java. It also traverses sale items and payments. A full-year range at a busy store can create large heap/latency spikes. Replace this with database-side aggregate queries or bounded chunks, and consider a smaller interactive range plus asynchronous export for longer periods. Preserve the current documented metric semantics while doing so.
- Inventory reporting loads all inventory balances for a location into memory and maps them to a response. This may be acceptable for the current single-shop catalogue, but it has no pagination or explicit product-count ceiling. Revisit if the catalogue grows substantially.
- The report date-range guard correctly rejects reversed dates and spans greater than 366 days between endpoints (up to 367 inclusive calendar dates). The controller binds ISO dates and catches domain validation errors. It does not catch malformed date syntax locally; Spring's standard 400 conversion is acceptable.
- POS return/void/exchange mutation endpoints consume action authorization before processing the operation. A failed business operation after consumption requires a fresh authorization token; this is a usability/security trade-off already recorded in the sensitive-action section, not a reason to weaken one-use authorization.
- POS checkout, returns and exchanges use `@Valid` DTOs. Return/exchange line collections and payment collections have explicit upper bounds. Checkout request validation is present, but the top-level `items` and `payments` lists in `PosCheckoutRequest` have no maximum `@Size`; the nested item/payment DTOs must also be checked for bounded values. Add a bounded line/payment count to prevent oversized authenticated requests and excessive transaction work, choosing a limit consistent with real optical-shop baskets.
- Shift open/history/current and cash movement operations have domain-level actor/shift checks. The shift history endpoint is role-gated to managers/admins; cashiers may read only their own current shift and cash movements. Staff-to-register assignment remains an explicitly deferred multi-branch control.

Recommended implementation: cap checkout item/payment list sizes with a shop-appropriate limit and add boundary tests; redesign report aggregation to avoid loading a full year's raw transactions; add pagination to shift history. Do not change report calculation definitions as part of a performance refactor without regression fixtures.

## Inventory, stock-count and procurement API resource review

- Inventory movement history accepts page/size and is delegated to a service with request bounds; low-stock and balance views are location-scoped. Stock adjustment and receipt DTOs use Bean Validation with positive IDs/quantities, bounded reason/idempotency strings, and adjustment delta bounds. Sensitive stock changes require fresh action authorization.
- Stock-count start and line DTOs validate positive location, bounded reason and nonnegative counted quantity. The count session is location-wide and deliberately freezes movement while counting. The controller relies on authenticated security context and service-level actor checks. Post/cancel consume action-specific fresh authorization.
- Purchase-order create, goods receipt and supplier return DTOs validate required fields and cap line arrays at 100; service-level idempotency and transactional stock movement behavior have separate MySQL regression coverage. This is a good baseline.
- Procurement list endpoints for suppliers, purchase orders, receipts and supplier returns return full lists without pagination. The cost report loads all matching receipts and supplier returns, then traverses all their lines and returns every entry. The report permits up to 367 calendar days. This is a scaling/availability risk as procurement history grows; paginate operational lists and make cost-report output paginated or export-based, while calculating totals in the database or bounded chunks.
- POS shift history similarly returns only the latest 100 records without pagination (recorded in the POS review). Inventory low-stock is also returned as a complete location list; likely acceptable for current shop size but should gain pagination if catalogue volume increases.
- Supplier create trims and checks case-insensitive duplicate names, but duplicate checks alone can race. Confirm the database has a case-insensitive/normalized unique constraint before relying on the controller's conflict response as a hard guarantee. Existing database uniqueness should be inspected before any migration.
- Procurement endpoints bind request DTOs rather than JPA entities, and mutation endpoints are protected by the admin route policy; submit/receive/supplier-return require fresh action authorization. No direct entity mass-assignment route was identified in these inspected controllers.
- Procurement cost report is explicitly a receipt/return unit-cost activity report, not AP, supplier invoice verification, landed cost, COGS or profit. Preserve that distinction in UI and exports.

Implementation priority: paginate procurement lists and cost-report entries before expanding transaction history; inspect and add a database-enforced supplier-name uniqueness guarantee only after checking existing schema/data. No schema change or behavior change was made in this review.

## Admin/customer order and catalogue API review

- Admin order list and customer aggregation both call `findAllWithItems()` and materialize every order and item in application memory. Neither endpoint paginates. This is a significant growth risk because order history and customer PII accumulate indefinitely. Add paginated order history and database-side customer aggregates; preserve stable sorting and existing response fields where the UI depends on them.
- Admin order response includes customer address/contact, Razorpay order/payment IDs, tracking token, and prescription details. It is behind `/api/admin/**` role protection, but access should be limited to staff who genuinely need the data. Avoid exposing tracking tokens and provider IDs in broad list responses; fetch sensitive detail only on demand and never log it.
- The `DELETE /api/admin/orders/clear` operation permanently deletes all orders after fresh-password `CLEAR_ORDERS` authorization. This is high impact: it removes customer/order history rather than archiving it. Do not expose this as routine maintenance; require a backup/export and documented retention decision before production use. Keep financial/POS records independent (this operation targets online orders).
- Order status and delivery update request DTOs have no Bean Validation constraints. Delivery strings are checked nonblank but have no maximum length or character/URL policy; status is checked against an allowlist. Add `@Valid`, bounded field lengths and explicit safe formatting rules after confirming courier/tracking provider requirements.
- Order status update contains a duplicated identical `if (!validTransition)` branch. The second branch is unreachable and can be removed as a no-behavior cleanup.
- SMS failure handling and checkout failure handling write exception messages to stderr. Provider exception messages may include identifiers or request details. Replace with structured, sanitized logs that retain internal order/correlation IDs and exception classes without raw provider messages, customer phone, tracking URL/token, payment signatures or payloads.
- Prescription JSON parse failures log the Jackson exception message. Treat prescription data as sensitive; log only order ID and exception class, never raw parser context that could echo input.
- Public product list is a customer-safe DTO, but public `GET /api/products/{id}` returns the JPA Product entity directly, including internal stock quantity, persistence version/timestamps and specifications. Use the same customer-safe DTO contract for product detail, or a dedicated detail DTO, and confirm frontend detail rendering before changing the response.
- Customer catalogue construction loads all products, all variants and all balances into memory on every request. There is no pagination or caching. This may be acceptable for the present catalogue size, but add paging/caching or query projections before the catalogue grows; preserve location-specific available-stock behavior and stock-count freeze semantics.
- Marketing banner, offer bar and website-image admin mutations bind JPA entities directly and return entities. This permits mass-assignment of persistence fields unless carefully reset; banner creation resets ID, but other controllers should use validated request/response DTOs. Public endpoints return entities too. Restrict public response fields and validate URL schemes, placement, color and date ranges before accepting admin content.
- Offer bar selection uses the first active row from an unordered repository result after date filtering. Overlapping active offers can therefore produce nondeterministic customer content. Define an explicit priority/order and overlap policy, then query deterministically.

No order API or customer-facing contract was changed during this audit. Prioritize paginated admin order access, PII-safe logging, public product detail DTO, and marketing DTO validation; do not alter the destructive clear operation without owner-approved retention policy.
